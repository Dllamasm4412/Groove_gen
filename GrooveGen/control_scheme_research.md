# Control scheme for closed-loop musical arousal/groove tracking (GrooveGen, TEI paper)

**Purpose of this document:** Recommend, with primary-literature support, what a per-bar closed-loop controller should manipulate to track a scripted heart-rate setpoint, given that GrooveGen is a *system/method* contribution (not an efficacy study). This document evaluates the four candidate schemes in the brief and gives one decisive recommendation.

---

## 1. Ranked arousal drivers (with citations + effect direction)

Music-emotion research converges on a small set of acoustic cues that reliably move *perceived/felt arousal* (activation), largely independent of *valence* (positive/negative) and largely additive rather than interactive. The two most-cited primary syntheses are Juslin & Västfjäll's mechanisms review and Gabrielsson & Lindström's structural-cue review:

- **Juslin & Västfjäll, "Emotional responses to music: The need to consider underlying mechanisms," *Behavioral and Brain Sciences* 31(5), 2008** — foundational account of *how* musical cues (not just *which* cues) generate emotion, via mechanisms like brain-stem reflex (loudness/rate) and rhythmic entrainment. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X08005293) · [Cambridge](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/identifier/S0140525X08005293/type/journal_article)
- **Gabrielsson & Lindström, "The role of structure in the musical expression of emotions,"** in *Handbook of Music and Emotion* (Juslin & Sloboda, eds.), Oxford University Press. [Oxford Academic](https://academic.oup.com/book/38621/chapter/335187128) · [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1093/ACPROF:OSO/9780199230143.003.0014) — the canonical tabulation of which structural features (tempo, mode, loudness, timbre, articulation, register/pitch) map to which perceived emotions, based on decades of performance-manipulation studies.

Ranked, with effect direction (↑ = higher value → higher arousal):

| Rank | Cue | Direction | Strength of evidence | Key primary source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Tempo** (events/min, BPM) | ↑ tempo → ↑ arousal | Very strong, highly replicated, largest single-cue effect size in factorial cue studies | Friberg, Bresin & Eerola, "Emotional expression in music: contribution, linearity, and additivity of primary musical cues," *Frontiers in Psychology* 4:487, 2013 — tempo ranked #2 overall behind mode, robust across genres. [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2013.00487/pdf) · [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3726864/). Also Ramos, Bigand & Bueno, "Manipulating Greek musical modes and tempo affects perceived musical emotion," *Braz J Med Biol Res* 44(2), 2011: tempo showed the single largest ANOVA effect on arousal (F(2,116)=268.6, p<.001). [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1590/S0100-879X2010007500148) |
| 2 | **Loudness / acoustic intensity** | ↑ loudness → ↑ arousal | Very strong; causally demonstrated with intensity-inversion manipulation | Schubert, Bailes & Dean, "Acoustic Intensity Causes Perceived Changes in Arousal Levels in Music," *PLOS ONE* 6(4):e18591, 2011 — inverting only the intensity contour inverted continuous arousal ratings, across multiple pieces of different styles. [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3080387/) · [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0018591). Corroborated by Gomez & Danuser (below). |
| 3 | **Timbral brightness / spectral centroid** | ↑ centroid ("brighter") → ↑ (tension) arousal | Strong for tension-arousal; more moderate/instrument-dependent for energy-arousal | Gomez & Danuser, "Relationships between musical structure and psychophysiological measures of emotion," *Emotion* 7(2):377–387, 2007. [APA/DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/1528-3542.7.2.377) — spectral centroid and sound-level were significant predictors of self-reported arousal and of respiratory/electrodermal arousal indices. Also Douglas, Vempala & McAdams, "Perception and Modeling of Affective Qualities of Musical Instrument Sounds across Pitch Registers," *Frontiers in Psychology* 8:153, 2017 — higher tension-arousal carried by brighter sounds (higher spectral centroid, slower spectral-slope decay). [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2017.00153/pdf) · [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5296353/) |
| 4 | **Rhythmic activity / articulation (note density, staccato vs. legato)** | ↑ density/more staccato → ↑ arousal | Moderate–strong as a *perceptual correlate*, but see Section 3 — density alone is a weaker, less controlled lever than tempo/loudness and is frequently confounded with tempo in stimulus sets | Friberg, Bresin & Eerola (2013), above — articulation ranked among the six primary cues but with smaller, more variable individual contribution than tempo/mode. [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2013.00487/pdf) |
| 5 | **Mode (major/minor)** | Minor → lower valence; effect on *arousal* is smaller/inconsistent, mode is primarily a **valence**, not arousal, cue | Strong for valence, weak/inconsistent for arousal | Friberg, Bresin & Eerola (2013) rank mode #1 overall for *emotional character* discrimination (happy/sad/scary/peaceful), but that is largely a valence axis; Ramos, Bigand & Bueno (2011) found mode's arousal contribution was much smaller than tempo's. [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2013.00487/pdf); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1590/S0100-879X2010007500148). Milne, Smit, Sarvasy & Dean, "Emotional responses in Papua New Guinea show negligible evidence for a universal effect of major versus minor music," *PLOS ONE* 17(6):e0269597, 2022, further shows mode's valence effect is culturally contingent, weakening its cross-cultural reliability as any kind of control signal. [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9242494/) |
| 6 | **Pitch height / register** | ↑ register → ↑ arousal (weaker, mixed with valence/tension) | Moderate; strongly interacts with timbre | Douglas, Vempala & McAdams (2017), above, and Friberg et al. (2013): register contributes but ranks below tempo/mode/dynamics. [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2017.00153/pdf) |

**Bottom line:** Tempo and loudness are the two most robust, most linear, most causally-demonstrated arousal levers in the literature. Timbral brightness is a credible third lever, particularly for "tension arousal." Mode is primarily a valence cue and should not be relied on as an arousal control variable. Raw note/event density is a real but weaker and noisier correlate (see Section 3).

Two additional syntheses worth citing in the paper's related-work section for framing:
- **Eerola & Vuoskoski line of work** on dimensional vs. discrete emotion models is best represented by Eerola & Vuoskoski's broader review tradition, e.g. the Cespedes-Guevara & Eerola constructionist account, "Music Communicates Affects, Not Basic Emotions," *Frontiers in Psychology* 9:215, 2018 [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.00215/pdf) · [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5836201/), and Eerola & Lennie's CODA model review, *Frontiers in Psychology* 13:822264, 2022. [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9043863/) These argue the valence/arousal circumplex is a reasonable simplification for engineering purposes even though richer discrete/constructionist models exist — useful for justifying a 1-D arousal control target in the Methods section.
- **Gomez & Danuser (2007)**, cited above, remains the most directly relevant "structure → psychophysiology" paper because it links musical *structural* features (not just self-report) to physiological arousal indices (skin conductance, respiration), which is the closest published analogue to using a physiological setpoint (HR) as your control target.

## 2. Groove vs. arousal: same construct, or separable?

**Separable.** Groove — operationalized as the "pleasurable urge to move" (PLUMM) — is empirically and theoretically distinguishable from generic arousal, though the two interact.

- **Janata, Tomic & Haberman, "Sensorimotor coupling in music and the psychology of the groove," *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General* 141(1), 2012.** [APA/DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0024208) — established groove as a quantifiable psychological construct tied to spontaneous sensorimotor engagement (tapping, head movement), not simply to felt activation level.
- **Witek, Clarke, Wallentin, Kringelbach & Vuust, "Syncopation, Body-Movement and Pleasure in Groove Music," *PLOS ONE* 9(4):e94446, 2014.** [PLOS](https://dx.plos.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0094446) · [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3989225/) — the landmark finding: **medium degrees of syncopation produce the highest ratings of wanting-to-move and pleasure; both low and high syncopation produce less.** This inverted-U is fundamentally different from arousal's largely monotonic relationship with tempo/loudness. Critically, rhythmic **entropy** (a density-adjacent complexity measure) was a *poor* predictor of wanting-to-move — syncopation structure mattered, not raw event count/complexity.
- **Replicated and refined:** Witek, Heggli, Vuust, Matthews & Penhune, "The sensation of groove is affected by the interaction of rhythmic and harmonic complexity," *PLOS ONE* 13(9):e0204539, 2018 [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6328141/); Stupacher, Wrede & Vuust, "A brief and efficient stimulus set to create the inverted U-shaped relationship between rhythmic complexity and the sensation of groove," *PLOS ONE* 17(5):e0266902, 2022 [PLOS](https://dx.plos.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0266902) · [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9119456/); and a further independent replication on OSF (Stupacher, Wrede & Vuust, 2021). [OSF](https://osf.io/n3yhq_v1) The inverted-U for **syncopation/rhythmic complexity → groove** is one of the most robustly replicated effects in this literature, including a null result for a *different* complexity operationalization (drum pattern complexity ratings) that helps delineate exactly which complexity measure matters: **Hösl, Kilchenmann, Witek, Senn & Bechtold, "Null effect of perceived drum pattern complexity on the experience of groove," *PLOS ONE* 19(11):e0311877, 2024** [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11567550/) — perceptually-rated "complexity" of drum patterns did *not* predict groove independent of syncopation, reinforcing that syncopation (metrical placement), not generic complexity/density, is the operative variable.
- **Theoretical synthesis:** Stupacher, Vander Elst, Vuust, Matthews & Pando-Naude, "The sweet spot between predictability and surprise: musical groove in brain, body, and social interactions," *Frontiers in Psychology* 13:906190, 2022. [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.906190/full) · [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9396343/) — groove sits at a predictive-processing "sweet spot" between predictability and surprise, a fundamentally different mechanism than the largely monotonic activation cues (tempo, loudness) that drive arousal.
- **Madison & Sioros, "What musicians do to induce the sensation of groove in simple and complex melodies, and how listeners perceive it," *Frontiers in Psychology* 5:894, 2014.** [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.00894/pdf) — identifies the specific rhythmical performance cues (event density interacting with regularity/beat salience) musicians use to signal groove, showing groove is actively "encoded" through a *combination* of structural cues, not a single scalar.
- **Microtiming (Matthews and related work):** microtiming/"participatory discrepancies" have a much weaker and less consistent effect than syncopation. Kilchenmann & Senn, "Microtiming in Swing and Funk affects the body movement behavior of music expert listeners," *Frontiers in Psychology* 6:1232, 2015 [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2015.01232/pdf) · [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4542135/), and the related Bullerjahn, Kilchenmann, Senn & von Georgi, "The Effect of Expert Performance Microtiming on Listeners' Experience of Groove in Swing or Funk Music," *Frontiers in Psychology* 7:1487, 2016 [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2016.01487/pdf), and "Rhythmic Density Affects Listeners' Emotional Response to Microtiming," *Frontiers in Psychology* 8:1709, 2017 [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2017.01709/pdf) — microtiming effects are small, inconsistent across expertise level, and interact with rhythmic density in ways that are not yet well enough characterized for engineering use.
- **Pleasure vs. movement dissociate:** Romkey, Foster, Dalla Bella, Matthews & Penhune, "The pleasurable urge to move to music is unchanged in people with musical anhedonia," *PLOS ONE* 20(1):e0312030, 2025 [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11706506/), and Witek, Matthews, Bechtold & Penhune, "Body maps of the sensation of musical groove," *PNAS Nexus*, 2025 [Oxford Academic](https://academic.oup.com/pnasnexus/article/doi/10.1093/pnasnexus/pgaf306/8262836) — show pleasure and wanting-to-move are dissociable sub-components (different body maps, different clinical populations affected), reinforcing that "groove" is its own multi-component construct, not a repackaging of arousal.

**Verdict:** Groove is a real, separable construct from arousal. It is governed chiefly by moderate syncopation / rhythmic complexity in an inverted-U relationship (not a monotonic one), and it is only weakly related to raw event density and microtiming. **If GrooveGen's outcome metric is heart rate (an arousal-linked physiological signal), groove is the wrong axis to build the primary controller around** — it does not have a monotonic dose-response relationship with a physiological setpoint, so a controller that increases syncopation will not reliably increase HR. Groove is nonetheless worth retaining as a secondary, intentionally *non-monotonic* design consideration (see recommendation) because moving away from moderate syncopation in either direction is exactly what makes the arousal-only version feel "off."

## 3. Verdict on raw density (keep / replace / augment)

**Replace as the primary controlled variable; retain (bounded) as a downstream implementation constraint, not as the control axis.**

Evidence against raw event-count density as a validated *arousal* lever:
- Witek et al. (2014) explicitly tested audio **entropy** (density/complexity) as a candidate predictor and found it was a **poor predictor** of wanting-to-move; syncopation (a structural/metrical property, not a count) was what mattered. [PLOS](https://dx.plos.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0094446)
- Gouyon, Madison, Sioros, Davies & Miron, "Syncopation creates the sensation of groove in synthesized music examples," *Frontiers in Psychology* 5:1036, 2014, manipulated syncopation and event density *together* and found syncopation, not density per se, drove groove ratings. [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.01036)
- Hösl, Kilchenmann, Senn & Bechtold, "Groove in drum patterns as a function of both rhythmic properties and listeners' attitudes," *PLOS ONE* 13(6):e0199604, 2018, tested **beat salience** as a candidate predictor and found it had **no significant effect** on groove ratings once other factors were modeled, while listener-level factors (style bias, familiarity) dominated variance. [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6025871/) This is a useful cautionary result: even *structured* rhythmic features do not trivially predict groove; unstructured density is even less likely to.
- The Preliminaries to a Psychological Model of Musical Groove review (Baldassarre, Rose, Alessandri, Hösl, Kilchenmann, Senn, Jerjen & Bechtold, *Frontiers in Psychology* 10:1228, 2019) explicitly separates "properties that add interest" (syncopation, rhythmic variability, microtiming) from "properties that emphasize regularity" (beat salience) as two different classes of candidate mechanism — density alone maps to neither cleanly. [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.01228/pdf) · [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6558102/)
- On the arousal side specifically, Friberg, Bresin & Eerola's factorial-cue study (2013) shows articulation/rhythmic-activity cues have measurable but comparatively **smaller and more genre-dependent** loadings on emotional ratings than tempo, mode, or dynamics. [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2013.00487/pdf)

Why this matches your observed symptom: raw density stuffing (int 3–20 events/bar with no structural template) is not a validated single-construct manipulation. It simultaneously (a) increases *perceptual* busyness/clutter without necessarily increasing syncopation (the actual groove driver) or loudness/tempo (the actual arousal drivers), and (b) has no floor on musical coherence — nothing in the density scalar enforces that added events land on metrically meaningful positions (backbeat, subdivision anchors), which is precisely what the "sounds incoherent/cluttered" observation predicts from this literature.

**Verdict: raw density should be replaced as the primary control variable.** It is not "wrong" as an *implementation detail* — density will still covary with intensity in any real drum arrangement — but it should be constrained inside a musically-designed structural template (backbeat salience preserved, subdivision added in prescribed patterns) rather than treated as a free scalar an optimizer can drive anywhere from 3 to 20. This directly supports Candidate Scheme 1 (structured intensity tiers) over the status quo.

## 4. Verdict on genre/style morphing

**Not defensible as the primary, single-variable arousal manipulation for a controlled TEI system paper.** It is a legitimate *compositional* idea but a poor *controlled-variable* idea, for reasons well documented in the confound literature:

- **Familiarity confound:** van den Bosch, Zatorre & Salimpoor, "Familiarity mediates the relationship between emotional arousal and pleasure during music listening," *Frontiers in Human Neuroscience* 7:534, 2013, show familiarity independently modulates arousal/pleasure ratings, even controlling for acoustic content. [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fnhum.2013.00534/pdf) · [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3763198/) Since salsa, pop, R&B/soul, and calm differ hugely in a given listener's prior exposure, morphing between them confounds "genre-driven arousal" with "familiarity-driven arousal/pleasure."
- **Preference/liking confound:** Francini & Jakubowski, "Differential effects of familiarity and emotional expression of musical cues on autobiographical memory properties," *Memory & Cognition* 51, 2023, and Levitin, Goldberg & Rentfrow's "structure of musical preferences" five-factor model (*Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* 100(6), 2011) both establish that genre identity is entangled with a listener's stable preference structure (e.g., "Mellow," "Unpretentious," "Intense" factors), which independently drives affective response regardless of acoustic energy content. [PMC (Francini)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10466948/); [PMC (Levitin et al.)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3138530/)
- **Genre-specific timing/style expertise confound:** Danielsen, Jacobsen, London, Nymoen, Langerød & Johansson, "Sounds familiar(?): Expertise with specific musical genres modulates timing perception and micro-level synchronization," *Attention, Perception, & Psychophysics* 84, 2021, shows that a listener's genre expertise changes low-level perceptual timing responses — meaning the *same* rhythmic content is processed differently depending on genre framing, which is a direct confound for any claim that "genre X caused arousal change Y." [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8888399/)
- **Everything co-varies at once:** by construction, a genre morph (e.g., calm → salsa) simultaneously changes tempo, instrumentation/timbre, syncopation pattern, harmonic idiom, and cultural/lyrical associations. This is the opposite of a controlled, single-DOF manipulation — it stacks nearly every variable in Sections 1–2 simultaneously, which is scientifically convenient for producing *a* strong effect but is fatal for a paper whose contribution is "a single scalar control law that a controller can act on predictably, per bar." A reviewer at TEI (or any HCI/psychology-adjacent venue) will immediately ask "was it the tempo change, the timbre change, or the genre-preference effect?" — and with a system that must also generalize control gains/latency estimates for a control loop, an un-decomposable multi-cue jump per bar makes system identification and stability analysis intractable.
- Additional physiological corroboration that tempo (a single extractable component of "genre energy") is what actually drives measured arousal responses even inside genre-labeled stimuli: Czepiel, Merrill, Fink, Fink, Tröndle & Wald-Fuhrmann, "Synchrony in the periphery: inter-subject correlation of physiological responses during live music concerts," *Scientific Reports* 11, 2021, found faster tempi (not style per se) predicted high physiological synchrony/arousal ratings, and only within already-familiar (Classical/Romantic) styles — i.e., style's apparent effect was tempo- and familiarity-mediated. [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8599424/)

**Verdict: genre morphing is confounded, not defensible as the primary controlled arousal variable.** It may be retained as a *high-level scene/preset selector chosen once per session by the user or narrative context* (e.g., "the salsa scene" vs. "the calm scene" as a style bed), but the *within-scene, per-bar, closed-loop* control signal that tracks the HR setpoint must be a single, decomposable, genre-agnostic parameter — not a jump between stylistically loaded, multi-cue-confounded states.

## 5. FINAL RECOMMENDATION

### Chosen scheme: **Candidate 1 — Structured Intensity Tiers**, implemented as a genre-agnostic arrangement-density ladder gated by backbeat/meter salience, with tempo and loudness as the two continuous trim variables. Reject density-as-free-scalar (status quo) and reject genre-morph-as-primary-lever. Groove/syncopation is retained but demoted to a fixed per-genre style parameter, not the closed-loop control variable.

**Why this wins over the other three candidates:**

- **vs. Candidate 2 (multi-parameter arousal bundle: tempo + loudness + brightness + rhythmic activity co-varied):** This is defensible from the emotion literature (Section 1) as an arousal manipulation, and tempo/loudness are individually the two best-supported levers (Schubert et al. 2011; Ramos et al. 2011). But co-varying 3–4 continuous parameters simultaneously from a single controller output reintroduces an identifiability problem for a *control-systems* paper: if HR tracking works, a reviewer cannot tell which parameter(s) did the work, and per-bar stability/gain analysis becomes multi-dimensional. It is scientifically stronger than density-alone or genre-morph, but it is not the cleanest *control signal*.
- **vs. Candidate 3 (separate groove axis + separate intensity axis):** Theoretically the most elegant, and well-grounded (Section 2) — groove and arousal are genuinely separable constructs, so a 2-DOF controller is conceptually honest. But it is over-engineered for the stated goal (track a scripted **HR** setpoint). HR is an arousal-linked signal; groove/syncopation has a non-monotonic (inverted-U), not dose-responsive, relationship to any physiological target, so a second control axis that cannot be driven monotonically toward a setpoint adds control complexity without adding controllability. Reserve this for a follow-up paper on subjective groove/danceability tracking; it is a confound-generator, not a simplification, for an HR-tracking demo.
- **vs. Candidate 4 (genre/style morph):** Rejected as primary lever per Section 4 — not decomposable, not reproducible, and introduces familiarity/preference/timbre/tempo confounds that undermine any claim about what the controller is actually doing.

### Concrete parameter definition for the engineer

Define a single ordinal **Arrangement Intensity Level, L ∈ {1, 2, 3, 4, 5}**, per bar, per the currently active genre bed. Each level is a **hand-authored, musically-complete arrangement state** for the drum track (and only the drum track — melody/harmony/bass remain the fixed symbolic bed), such that:

- **L1 (baseline):** downbeat + backbeat only (kick on 1/3, snare or genre-equivalent backbeat on 2/4 or its clave/tumbao equivalent for salsa). Minimal ride/hat, quarter-note pulse only.
- **L2:** add offbeat hi-hat/shaker subdivision (8th-note layer) while keeping backbeat salience identical to L1 (same velocity/timbre on 2/4).
- **L3:** add 16th-note subdivision layer on hats/percussion; backbeat still fixed and salient; introduce a small, fixed, genre-idiomatic syncopation pattern (e.g., a single anticipated snare or clave hit) at a **moderate, not maximal** syncopation level, consistent with the inverted-U evidence (Witek et al. 2014) — i.e., do not push toward the high-syncopation end even at high L.
- **L4:** add secondary percussion voice (e.g., congas/toms/claps) plus loudness trim +Δ dB and a small tempo nudge (+Δ BPM, capped) — this is where the continuous arousal trims from Section 1 (tempo, loudness) are folded in on top of the structural tier.
- **L5 (max):** full arrangement density + fill/turnaround at bar boundaries + max (capped) loudness/tempo trim.

Formally: **HR-tracking control law drives L (ordinal, coherent, backbeat-preserving) each bar; within each L, continuous trims for loudness (dB) and tempo (BPM, small range, e.g. ±6%) are the fine-control actuators; timbral brightness (filter cutoff / EQ tilt on the drum bus) is an optional third trim.** Backbeat position, meter, and syncopation *pattern* at each level are fixed at design time per genre — the controller never invents new syncopation or reorders subdivisions on the fly, and never operates as a free event-count scalar. Density is therefore not eliminated; it is **quantized and structurally gated**, so every level is a complete, coherent, pre-validated arrangement rather than an interpolated pile of extra notes.

This makes the controller output a genuinely low-dimensional, monotonic-in-expected-arousal signal (L, plus two small bounded trims) that a PID/MPC-style loop can drive toward a scripted HR setpoint with clear, reportable gain/latency behavior per bar — while remaining musically coherent because every discrete state was authored, not algorithmically stuffed.

### How to describe it in the paper's Methods (2–3 sentences)

> "The controller manipulates a single ordinal *arrangement intensity* parameter (levels 1–5) per bar, where each level corresponds to a pre-composed, backbeat-preserving drum arrangement state that escalates rhythmic subdivision, percussion layering, and fill density in a musically validated progression, rather than an unconstrained event-count scalar; small bounded trims to loudness and tempo are applied continuously within each level as secondary actuators. This design follows evidence that tempo and loudness are the most robust drivers of perceived arousal (Gomez & Danuser, 2007; Schubert, Bailes, & Dean, 2011) and that raw rhythmic event density is a poor predictor of perceptual outcomes such as groove and perceived complexity compared to structured metrical placement (Witek et al., 2014; Hösl et al., 2018, 2024), while a distinct sensorimotor 'groove' construct governed by moderate syncopation (Witek et al., 2014; Janata et al., 2012) is held fixed per level rather than used as the closed-loop control variable. We do not manipulate genre/style identity within a trial, as genre morphing confounds tempo, timbre, familiarity, and preference (van den Bosch et al., 2013; Levitin et al., 2011), which would make the source of any observed heart-rate tracking response non-identifiable."

### Trade-offs to state explicitly in the paper (be honest)

1. **Discretization cost:** a 5-level ordinal controller has coarser resolution than a continuous density scalar; if fine-grained HR tracking requires finer steps, add the continuous loudness/tempo trims (already in the design) rather than subdividing L further — more than ~5–7 authored levels reintroduces the authoring burden the current design escapes.
2. **Authoring burden vs. generality:** structured tiers must be hand-designed per genre (salsa, pop, R&B/soul, calm), which is more production work up front than a scalar, but this is exactly what buys musical coherence — name this explicitly as a deliberate cost/benefit trade rather than an oversight.
3. **Not a groove-optimization system:** by fixing syncopation per level, GrooveGen is explicitly *not* claiming to maximize danceability/groove — only arousal-linked intensity. If reviewers expect a groove claim, state clearly that groove was intentionally held constant as a confound-control measure, citing Witek et al. (2014) and Janata et al. (2012) for why the two constructs cannot share one control axis.
4. **Mode/pitch height excluded:** consistent with Section 1, mode and pitch height are not used as control variables because they are weaker and more valence-oriented/culturally contingent (Milne et al., 2022) — flag this so the paper doesn't appear to have overlooked classic cues, it deliberately deprioritized them.
